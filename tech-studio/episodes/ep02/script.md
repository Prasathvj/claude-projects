## HOOK ★ Max
1. Right now, in a factory in Japan, the lights are off.
2. Nobody is inside. And the machines are still building… more machines.
3. FANUC has run factories like this since 2001. Its robots can work for up to thirty days without a human checking on them.
4. So here's the question everyone is quietly asking: if robots can work in the dark, do they still need us?
5. The answer is stranger than yes or no. And it starts with a machine you probably used last week.

## ACT 1: How many robots are there?
6. First, the scale. At the end of 2025, about five million industrial robots were working in factories around the world. A new record.
7. In 2025 alone, factories installed more than six hundred thousand new ones.
8. China installed fifty-nine percent of them. More than the rest of the world combined.
9. And India? About ten thousand five hundred in a single year. Another record, and sixth in the world.
10. Outside factories, Amazon says it now runs more than one million robots across its warehouse network.
11. Internal documents reported by The New York Times suggest automation could let Amazon avoid hiring about six hundred thousand people by 2033.
12. Amazon says those papers reflect one team, not its hiring plan.
13. So yes, the robots are real, and they're multiplying. But here's what the headlines miss.

## ACT 2: What robots still can't do
14. Robots are brilliant at things we find hard… and terrible at things we find easy.
15. A robot arm can weld a car door in seconds, exactly the same way, thousands of times a day.
16. But ask it to fold a towel, or find one sock in a messy laundry basket, and it struggles.
17. Scientists call this Moravec's paradox. Seeing, grasping, walking through a cluttered room: the skills evolution spent millions of years building are the hardest ones to copy.
18. That's why the new humanoid robots, the ones shaped like people, still do narrow jobs. Moving boxes. Loading metal parts. Carrying totes.
19. Figure's humanoids spent months loading sheet-metal parts at a BMW plant in the US. Agility says its robot, Digit, has moved more than a hundred thousand totes in a warehouse.
20. Impressive. But a long way from replacing a mechanic, a nurse or an electrician.

## ACT 3: The ATM story ★ Max
21. Now, that machine you used last week. The ATM.
22. When ATMs arrived, people said bank tellers were finished. A machine that hands out cash all day, with no lunch break.
23. What actually happened? ATMs made each branch cheaper to run. So banks opened more branches.
24. Each branch needed fewer tellers. But there were many more branches.
25. In the US, teller jobs didn't collapse. By 2018 there were more tellers than in 2000.
26. And the job changed. Less counting cash. More helping customers, selling loans, solving problems.
27. Economists see this pattern again and again: machines take over tasks, not whole jobs.
28. But here's the twist. The story didn't end there.
29. Today, the World Economic Forum lists bank tellers among the fastest-declining jobs on the planet. Not because of the ATM. Because of the phone in your pocket.
30. In India you already know what that looks like: you scan a QR code, and you never meet a cashier at all.
31. So the lesson isn't "technology never takes jobs". It's this: the first machine rarely kills a job. The next one can.

## ACT 4: What the big studies say
32. So what do the big studies predict?
33. The World Economic Forum surveyed more than a thousand of the world's largest employers.
34. Their forecast: by 2030, about ninety-two million jobs displaced… and one hundred and seventy million new ones created.
35. That's a net gain of seventy-eight million jobs. But a global total hides millions of people forced to switch careers.
36. The roles shrinking fastest: cashiers, clerks, data entry, bank tellers.
37. The roles growing fastest: AI and machine learning specialists, big data specialists, fintech engineers.
38. And the biggest growth in raw numbers? Not tech at all. Farmworkers, delivery drivers, construction workers.
39. The IMF adds another angle: almost forty percent of jobs worldwide are exposed to AI. In rich countries, about sixty percent.
40. Exposed doesn't mean replaced. The IMF says roughly half of those jobs could benefit, because AI makes the worker more productive.
41. The other half could see less hiring and lower pay.

## ACT 5: What it means for India ★ Max
42. So what does this mean for India?
43. India's factories are automating fast, mostly car plants. Automotive takes almost half of India's new industrial robots.
44. But India's biggest job engine isn't the assembly line. It's services. IT, customer support, back office.
45. And that's exactly where AI, not robots, is moving in. Software that writes code, answers tickets and reads documents.
46. It's the ATM pattern again: the routine tasks go first. Checking, copying, sorting, repeating.
47. The work that stays is the work around the machine: designing it, fixing it, checking what it produces, and dealing with people.

## PAYOFF ★ Max
48. So, will robots replace human jobs?
49. Some, yes. A job that is one repetitive task from start to finish is in real danger.
50. But most jobs are a bundle of tasks. Machines take the predictable ones, and leave the messy, human ones.
51. Remember that dark factory in Japan? Someone designed those robots. Someone programs them. And when one breaks down at three in the morning, a human still gets the call.
52. So the better question isn't "Will a robot take my job?" It's "Which part of my job could a robot do… and what will I do instead?"
53. Answer that before your boss does, and you're already ahead.
