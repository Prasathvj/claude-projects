## 1. Hook ★ Max
VISUAL: Max at a desk, typing. A code editor fills itself line by line. A counter climbs from 25% to 75%; label "Google: share of new code generated by AI". Max slowly stops typing and leans back.
1. At Google, AI now generates about seventy-five percent of all new code. Two years ago, it was about a quarter.
2. So if a machine is writing three out of every four lines, here's the obvious question. What happens to the people who used to write them?
3. Search "will AI replace software engineers", and you'll find two camps. One says coding is dead. The other says nothing will change.
4. Both are wrong. And the real data tells a much stranger story, one that matters a lot if you work in IT, or you're planning to.
5. In the next few minutes, we'll look at what AI can actually do today, who is really losing jobs, what India's IT giants are doing, and the skills that keep you on the right side of this shift.

## 2. What "75%" actually means
VISUAL: big "75%" splits into small print: "of NEW code" · "suggested by AI" · "reviewed and accepted by engineers". Bit types fast; a human hand presses "Approve".
6. First, let's decode that seventy-five percent, because it doesn't mean what most headlines suggest.
7. Google's CEO said it refers to new code, generated by AI and then reviewed and accepted by engineers. Not the whole codebase. And not code that ships without a human checking it.
8. Think of it like autocomplete on your phone. If your keyboard suggests three out of four words in a message, you still decided what the message says.
9. Microsoft gave a similar picture. Satya Nadella said AI writes twenty to thirty percent of the code in some of its projects.
10. So AI is clearly typing a lot. But typing was never the hardest part of a software engineer's job.

## 3. We've heard this before
VISUAL: sepia flashback. An IBM 704 mainframe, punch cards, then a 1954 report card with the highlighted line "should virtually eliminate coding and debugging". Stamp: "IBM, 1954".
11. And here's the twist. Programmers have heard this exact prediction before. Seventy years ago.
12. In 1954, IBM published the first report on a new language called FORTRAN. It promised that FORTRAN would "virtually eliminate coding and debugging".
13. Instead of writing raw machine instructions, you could write something close to a maths formula, and the computer would translate it for you.
14. Did programmers disappear? The opposite. Making programming easier made software cheaper, so the world wanted far more of it.
VISUAL: line chart of "software jobs" rising through the decades; labels FORTRAN, PCs, the internet, smartphones, cloud.
15. Every big jump since then, high-level languages, the internet, cloud platforms, made each programmer more productive. And each time, the total number of programmers grew.
16. Economists have a name for this. When something gets cheaper, we often end up using much more of it. So the real question isn't whether AI makes coding cheaper. It's who gets the new work.

## 4. Who is actually losing ★ Max
VISUAL: a ladder labelled "Career". The top rungs are solid; the bottom rung cracks and falls away. Max reaches for it.
17. And this is where the data gets uncomfortable.
18. Researchers at Stanford tracked payroll records from millions of American workers. Their latest figures show something striking.
19. Software developers aged twenty-two to twenty-five are employed almost twenty percent less than a few years ago, while developers over thirty actually grew.
VISUAL: two bars. "Age 22–25: down ~20%" in red; "Age 30+: growing" in green. Source tag: Stanford Digital Economy Lab.
20. Job postings tell the same story. On Indeed in the US, only about four and a half percent of software job posts in early twenty twenty-six were entry-level. That's the lowest share of any field Indeed tracks.
21. And most of the recent rebound in software hiring, around seventy percent of it, came from senior roles.
22. So AI isn't replacing software engineers. It's replacing the first rung of the ladder. The simple tickets, the boilerplate, the small bug fixes that juniors used to learn on.
23. The researchers are careful to say this is a pattern, not proof that AI alone caused it. Interest rates and over-hiring during the pandemic play a part too. But the direction is hard to ignore.

## 5. What about India?
VISUAL: India map; logos replaced by neutral building icons labelled "Top 5 IT firms". Two counters side by side: "Revenue +6.1%" and "Jobs +2.3%".
24. Now let's bring it home, because India runs one of the biggest software workforces on the planet.
25. According to Nasscom, the Indian tech industry's revenue grew about six percent last financial year, but jobs grew only about two percent.
26. In other words, the industry is earning more without hiring at the same speed. That gap is exactly what automation looks like on a balance sheet.
27. TCS, India's largest IT company, cut more than twelve thousand roles in twenty twenty-five as part of a restructuring, and its net headcount fell by over twenty-three thousand in the year.
28. But the hiring hasn't stopped. TCS and Infosys together still plan around forty-five thousand fresher hires this year. And within IT, hiring for AI and machine learning roles is growing.
VISUAL: a funnel. Wide top "Generic coding roles", narrow; a new pipe branching off "AI, cloud, data roles", widening.
29. So the door isn't closed. It's moving. Fewer seats for "write this simple module", more for "build and check AI-powered systems".

## 6. Does AI actually make developers faster?
VISUAL: a race. Max with AI versus Max without AI. A stopwatch. The "with AI" Max starts fast, then gets stuck in a pile of red error messages.
30. Here's a result that surprised almost everyone, including the developers in it.
31. In twenty twenty-five, a research group called METR ran a proper experiment. Experienced open-source developers worked on their own projects, sometimes with AI tools, sometimes without.
32. Before starting, they expected AI to make them about twenty-four percent faster.
33. The measured result? With AI, they were nineteen percent slower. And afterwards, they still believed AI had sped them up.
34. Why? Reviewing, correcting and re-prompting AI output takes time, especially in large, complex code you already know well.
35. To be fair, that study used early twenty twenty-five tools, and the tools have improved a lot since. But it shows something important. AI writes code fast. Making sure that code is right is still human work.

## 7. The jobs AI can't take (yet) ★ Max
VISUAL: Max at a whiteboard. Bit hands him code; Max circles a bug. Icons appear one by one: a speech bubble (requirements), a shield (security), a map (architecture), a magnifier (review).
36. So what part of the job is safe? Mostly the parts that were never about typing.
37. Understanding what the customer actually needs, when they can't explain it clearly.
38. Designing how a big system fits together, so it doesn't collapse when a million users show up.
39. Judging whether AI-written code is secure, because AI will confidently write code with security holes in it.
40. And taking responsibility when something breaks at two in the morning. An AI can suggest a fix. It can't be accountable for it.
41. Notice the pattern. AI is great at answering "how do I write this?" Humans are still needed for "what should we build, and is it right?"
42. That's also why the official forecasts are still positive. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics expects software developer jobs to grow about sixteen percent over the next ten years, much faster than average.

## 8. What to do if you're in IT ★ Max
VISUAL: checklist UI with four items; Max ticks each, Bit gives a thumbs-up.
43. So if you're a developer, or you want to be one, here's how to stay on the right side of this.
44. One. Use AI tools every day, but treat them like a fast junior teammate. Let them draft. You review, test and decide.
45. Two. Climb above syntax. Learn system design, debugging and reading other people's code. Those skills get more valuable as AI writes more of the code.
46. Three. Learn the basics of how AI systems work, and how they fail. Companies need people who can build with AI safely.
47. Four. If you're a fresher, build real projects that show judgement, not just a certificate. Something deployed, used by real people, and explained clearly.
VISUAL: a fresher's portfolio card: "Live app · 200 users · bugs fixed · write-up" next to a plain "Certificate" card that fades.

## 9. Close ★ Max
VISUAL: Max and Bit side by side at one desk. Bit types, Max points at the screen and they high-five. End screen.
48. So, will AI replace software engineers? Not the ones who learn to work with it.
49. But it is changing who gets hired, and what they're hired to do. And that change is happening right now, starting with the very first rung of the ladder.
50. Tell me in the comments: do you use AI at work? Is it making your job easier, or harder?
