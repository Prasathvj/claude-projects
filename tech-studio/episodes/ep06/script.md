## 1. Hook ★ Max
PAPER: a paper chip (AI→"Q") drops onto a desk; a paper clock spins 5 minutes. Next to it a stack of paper calendars grows taller and taller off the top of the screen. VOX board: "5 MINUTES vs 10,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 YEARS".
1. In under five minutes, a chip the size of a postage stamp finished a calculation that would take one of the world's fastest supercomputers ten septillion years.
2. That's a one followed by twenty-five zeros. Far longer than the universe has existed.
3. The chip is Google's Willow, a quantum computer. And it sounds like magic, which is exactly why so much of what you hear about quantum computing is wrong.
4. So in the next few minutes, let's decode what a quantum computer really is, why it has to be colder than outer space, what it can and can't do, and why your bank is already preparing for it.

## 2. Bits vs qubits
PAPER: a row of paper light switches/coins: each shows 0 or 1. Then one coin spins on its edge (stop-motion blur), showing both faces.
5. Start with the computer you're using right now. Everything it does comes down to bits, tiny switches that are either zero or one.
6. Every photo, every video, every message is just billions of those switches flipping on and off, extremely fast.
7. A quantum computer uses qubits instead. And a qubit can be in a mix of zero and one at the same time, a state called superposition.
8. A simple way to picture it: a normal bit is a coin lying flat, heads or tails. A qubit is a coin still spinning in the air. Only when you look does it land on one side.
9. Qubits can also be linked together, so that measuring one instantly tells you something about another. That's called entanglement.
VOX board: "2 qubits → 4 states at once · 10 → 1,024 · 300 → more states than atoms in the observable universe" (labelled: number of possible states, 2^n).
10. Here's why that matters. Two qubits can hold a blend of four possibilities. Ten can hold over a thousand. Three hundred qubits can hold more possibilities than there are atoms in the observable universe.

## 3. The biggest myth ★ Max
PAPER: a paper maze; a paper Bit tries every path at once with many ghost copies → big red CROSS stamp "MYTH". Then: waves (paper ripples) where wrong paths cancel and the right path glows.
11. Now, the most common explanation you'll hear is that a quantum computer tries every answer at the same time. That's the myth.
12. If it simply tried everything at once, you'd still only get one random answer when you look. That's useless.
13. The real trick is called interference. Like waves in water, the possibilities can add up or cancel out.
14. A quantum algorithm is a clever recipe that makes the wrong answers cancel each other out, and the right answer get stronger, so that when you finally measure, you're likely to see the right one.
15. That's also why quantum computers aren't faster at everything. They only win on problems where someone has found a recipe like that.

## 4. Colder than space
PAPER: a tall paper "chandelier" fridge (stacked golden plates/tubes made from blocks) with a thermometer strip dropping 4 K → 0.1 K → 0.01 K. A paper planet/star field labelled "space: 2.7 K". VOX: "≈ 0.01 K: about 270× colder than deep space".
16. So why does a quantum computer look like a giant golden chandelier?
17. Because qubits are incredibly fragile. The tiniest bit of heat, vibration or stray signal knocks them out of their delicate state. Physicists call this noise.
18. So most quantum chips, including Google's, sit inside special refrigerators that cool them to around ten thousandths of a degree above absolute zero.
19. Deep space is about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero. So the inside of a quantum computer is roughly two hundred and seventy times colder than outer space.

## 5. Errors: the real battle
PAPER: a row of paper qubits (spinning coins) where some fall flat and crack (red cross) as a clock ticks. Then a group of many coins holding hands forms one big "logical" coin with a green check.
20. Even when they're that cold, qubits make mistakes. A lot of them. That's been the biggest wall in quantum computing for decades.
21. The fix is called error correction: you combine many physical qubits into one reliable "logical" qubit, so the group can catch and correct individual mistakes.
22. The problem was that adding more qubits usually added more errors too.
23. Willow's real breakthrough wasn't that five-minute race. It's that as Google made its groups of qubits bigger, the error rate went down instead of up. That's a key milestone researchers had been chasing for almost thirty years.
VOX board: "Google Quantum Echoes (Oct 2025): 13,000× faster than a top supercomputer · verifiable · published in Nature".
24. And in twenty twenty-five, Google showed an algorithm called Quantum Echoes running thirteen thousand times faster than one of the best supercomputers, on a result other machines can check.

## 6. What it's actually good for
PAPER: four paper cards drop in a row, each with a prop: molecule/flask (book+bulb) "new medicines & materials", gear "complex optimisation", globe "weather & physics simulation", lock "breaking today's encryption" (red).
25. So what will quantum computers actually be useful for? Not for browsing, gaming or running ChatGPT faster.
26. Their best targets are problems that are quantum by nature. Simulating molecules to design new medicines, better batteries and new materials.
27. They may also help with certain hard optimisation and simulation problems, though researchers are still debating how big that advantage will be.
28. And then there's the one that worries governments and banks: breaking the encryption that protects almost everything online.

## 7. Your bank is already preparing ★ Max
PAPER: a paper padlock on an envelope; a shady paper figure with a sack collects envelopes into a box labelled "LATER". VOX: NIST timeline "Aug 2024: first post-quantum standards · after 2030: old encryption deprecated · after 2035: disallowed".
29. Much of today's online security, from your UPI payments to your WhatsApp messages, relies on maths problems that normal computers would take practically forever to crack.
30. A large enough, error-corrected quantum computer could solve some of those problems dramatically faster.
31. Nobody has built that machine yet. Official estimates put it somewhere in the twenty-thirties, and nobody knows the exact date.
32. But there's a catch called "harvest now, decrypt later". Someone can steal encrypted data today and simply wait for a quantum computer to unlock it years from now.
33. That's why in twenty twenty-four, the US standards agency NIST published the first "post-quantum" encryption standards, and set a plan to phase out vulnerable encryption after twenty thirty.
34. Tech companies and banks have already started switching. If your apps update their security quietly over the next few years, this is one big reason why.

## 8. What about India?
PAPER: paper map-ish India shape (use globe close-up) with a long paper cable/arrow strip "1,000+ km"; a small paper chip labelled 25. VOX board: "National Quantum Mission · ₹6,003 crore · 2023–2031".
35. India is in this race too. In twenty twenty-three, the government approved the National Quantum Mission, with six thousand and three crore rupees over eight years.
36. The goal is to build quantum computers with up to a thousand qubits by around twenty thirty-one.
37. Today, one of India's most powerful machines, built by the Bengaluru startup QpiAI, has twenty-five qubits. That's far smaller than Google's, but it's a start.
38. And India is ahead on quantum-secure communication, with more than a thousand kilometres of quantum-secure links already set up.

## 9. Will you own one? ★ Max
PAPER: Max's paper laptop connects with a paper arrow to a paper cloud, which connects to the paper "chandelier" fridge. Bit gives a thumbs up (bob).
39. So, will you ever have a quantum computer at home? Almost certainly not. They're huge, fragile and built for very specific problems.
40. Instead, you'll use them the way you use most powerful computers today: through the cloud, often without even knowing it.
41. Some companies already let students and developers run small programs on real quantum chips online, many for free.
42. Quantum computers won't replace your laptop. They'll work alongside regular computers, as special tools for a few incredibly hard problems.

## 10. Close ★ Max
PAPER: the spinning coin finally lands, showing the TechDecoded logo; Bit and Max high-five. End screen.
43. So, a quantum computer isn't a faster laptop, and it doesn't try every answer at once. It's a completely different machine, using the strange rules of physics to solve a few problems no normal computer ever could.
44. The hype is real in some places, and wildly overblown in others. Now you know which is which.
