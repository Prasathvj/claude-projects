November twenty-second, nineteen eighty-seven. Chicago.
Millions of people are watching the evening sports news.
At fourteen minutes past nine, the screen goes black.
Then someone else appears. Someone who was never supposed to be there.
And to this day, nobody knows who it was.
This is the story of a TV hijack that has stayed unsolved for almost forty years.
And of how one person, with homemade equipment, took over two of the biggest TV stations in America.
Let's open the case file.
It's a Sunday night on WGN, channel nine. The sports anchor is reading the day's highlights.
Suddenly, the picture flickers, and the newsroom disappears.
In its place, a figure in a rubber mask is rocking from side to side.
Behind him, a sheet of metal is spinning, flashing light like a broken screen.
The mask copies Max Headroom, a fake computer-generated TV character who was famous at the time. No relation to our Max, I promise.
There's no sound. Just a buzzing hum, and that face.
At the station, engineers scramble. Within about half a minute, they change the frequency of their signal, and the intruder is gone.
Back in the studio, the anchor looks into the camera and says, "If you're wondering what's happened, so am I."
For two hours, Chicago thinks it's over. It isn't.
At about ten past eleven, on a different station, WTTW, people are watching Doctor Who.
And the masked figure comes back. This time, with sound.
His voice is distorted, almost robotic. He laughs, mumbles, and mocks the station that threw him off the air earlier.
He holds up a soft drink can and repeats an advertising slogan. He hums a cartoon theme song.
He says something strange: that his brother has "the other one".
And the strangest part is how it ends. Someone else steps into the frame, holding a flyswatter.
Then, after about ninety seconds, the screen cuts back to Doctor Who, as if nothing happened.
And this time, nobody could stop him. There were no engineers at the transmitter that night.
So how do you hijack a TV station from the outside?
In nineteen eighty-seven, a TV station's studio and its transmitter were often in different places.
The studio sent its picture to a tall tower using a narrow beam of microwave radio, a bit like an invisible cable in the air.
WGN's transmitter sat on top of the John Hancock Center. WTTW's was on top of the Sears Tower.
The tower simply broadcast whatever arrived on that beam.
So if someone aimed a stronger signal at the same receiver, the tower would broadcast them instead.
That isn't easy. You need a transmitter, a dish, and a clear line of sight to a skyscraper, plus serious knowledge of broadcast equipment.
In other words, this wasn't a prank by a random kid. It was someone who knew exactly how TV worked.
And that should have made him easy to catch. Because just one year earlier, someone else tried something similar.
In April nineteen eighty-six, a message appeared over a movie on HBO: "Good evening HBO, from Captain Midnight."
He was protesting HBO's twelve dollar ninety-five monthly fee.
Investigators traced the satellite signal back to a technician in Florida, named John MacDougall.
He pleaded guilty, paid a five thousand dollar fine, and got a year of probation.
Captain Midnight was caught. So why wasn't the man in the mask?
The FCC investigated. But the trail went cold almost immediately.
A microwave signal leaves no fingerprints. Once it stops, there is nothing left to trace.
The intruder never asked for money, never made a demand, and never did it again.
And as one investigator later put it, the hijacker wasn't a danger to public safety. So the case was never a priority.
So let's look at the clues ourselves.
Clue one. He hit two different stations on two different towers, on the same night. He knew where both beams were, and how to reach them.
Clue two. The mask and the spinning metal were cheap, almost homemade. But the transmission was not. Whoever did this put their money into the technology, not the costume.
Clue three. In the second broadcast, he mocked WGN, the station that had cut him off. So he was probably watching the first hijack live, from somewhere in the city.
Clue four. He talked about his brother. Was there a second person helping him? The one holding the flyswatter, maybe?
Every clue points to an insider. Someone who understood broadcast engineering. And yet, nobody ever came forward.
Over the years, people have tried to solve it themselves.
In twenty ten, a post on Reddit claimed the culprits came from Chicago's local hacker scene. People who knew the equipment, and knew the city.
But no one has ever been named by police. No suspect, no witness, no confession.
And here's the part that should worry you more than a man in a mask.
In February twenty thirteen, TV viewers in Montana saw an emergency alert interrupt their show.
It warned that the bodies of the dead were rising from their graves and attacking the living.
It was a hack. Someone had broken into the stations' Emergency Alert System equipment.
How? Some of those machines were still using the default passwords printed in their public user manuals.
The people behind the zombie alert were never caught either.
Think about that. A message millions of people would trust, protected by a password anyone could look up.
And that's the real lesson of this case. Most hacks don't need genius. They need one door that nobody locked.
So go check one thing tonight: your home Wi-Fi router. If it still uses the default admin password, change it.
Today, TV signals are digital and far better protected. A hijack like nineteen eighty-seven would be much harder.
But the man in the mask is still out there somewhere. Or maybe he isn't.
