At 1:21 in the morning, a plane with two hundred and thirty nine people on board vanished from air traffic control. But a satellite kept hearing it for almost seven more hours.
March 8th, 2014. Malaysia Airlines flight 370, Kuala Lumpur to Beijing.
The last words from the cockpit: Good night, Malaysian three seven zero. Moments later, its transponder went dark.
Military radar saw something no one expected. The plane turned back, crossed Malaysia, and flew out over the sea.
Then it slipped beyond radar range. Gone.
But a satellite kept sending automatic pings. And the plane kept answering.
No messages. No voices. Just seven tiny handshakes, each carrying a time stamp and a shift in frequency.
From those pings alone, engineers traced a path ending deep in the southern Indian Ocean.
In 2015, a piece of its wing washed up on Reunion Island, near Africa.
Searches have scanned over a hundred thousand square kilometers of seabed. The latest ended in 2026. Nothing.
Who turned the plane around, and why? Still, no one knows.
