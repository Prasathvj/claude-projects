## 1. Hook ★ Max
VISUAL: PHONE with orange mic dot + Instagram logo; paper running shoe; dinner (fork & knife icons); "ADS" cards raining in.
1. You mention running shoes to a friend over dinner. You never search for them. And the next morning, your Instagram is full of running shoe ads.
2. It feels like proof. Your phone heard you.
3. Millions of people believe this. Some put tape over their microphones. Some say a product name out loud, just to see if the ads follow.
4. So let's decode it properly: the scientists who tested it, the marketing company that bragged about doing it, the times phones really did record people, and how ads seem to read your mind without hearing a single word.

## 2. Scientists tested it
VISUAL: Northeastern photo-print card, Android logo, counter 0→17,000 apps, data packets flying, "AUDIO: 0" stamp; screen-record icon + GoPuff card.
5. In twenty eighteen, researchers at Northeastern University in Boston set out to catch phones in the act.
6. They used automated software to run more than seventeen thousand popular Android apps, including Facebook's own apps, and they captured every piece of data those apps sent out.
7. They were looking for audio leaving the phone. They found none. Not one app secretly switched on the microphone and sent recordings.
8. The researchers were careful to say this doesn't prove it never happens. But it was one of the biggest tests of its kind, and it came up empty.
9. And they did find something creepy. Some apps were quietly recording the screen and sending videos of what users were doing to other companies.
10. One food delivery app, GoPuff, was sending screen recordings to an analytics firm without telling users. It stopped after the researchers got in touch. No microphone needed.
11. Facebook has also said, on the record, since twenty sixteen, that it does not use your phone's microphone to choose your ads.

## 3. The leak ★ Max
VISUAL: Facebook 2016 quote clipping (Vox); 404 Media newspaper clipping "Active Listening"; Google logo with "removed" stamp; Amazon logo "not us"; "SALES DECK ≠ EVIDENCE".
12. Then, in twenty twenty-four, a leak seemed to prove the opposite.
13. The news site 404 Media published a sales pitch deck from Cox Media Group, a big American marketing company. It advertised something called "Active Listening".
14. The deck claimed it could use voice data from smart devices to find people who were talking about products, and then target those people with ads.
15. Google removed the company from its advertising partner program. Amazon said it had nothing to do with it.
16. But a sales deck is not evidence. Nobody has shown that this product worked as advertised, or that it ever ran at any real scale.

## 4. When phones DID record
VISUAL: "Hey Siri / Hey Google" speech bubbles, Apple + Google logos, $95M and $68M counters, "accidental" stamp.
17. There is one place where phones really did record people, though. Voice assistants.
18. Your phone listens for a wake word, like "Hey Siri" or "Hey Google". Sometimes it mishears, wakes up, and records a private conversation by mistake.
19. In twenty twenty-five, Apple agreed to pay ninety-five million dollars to settle a US lawsuit over accidental Siri recordings. Google later agreed to pay sixty-eight million dollars over the same problem with Google Assistant. Neither company admitted wrongdoing.
20. Those recordings were accidents, not an advertising machine. But they show the risk is real.

## 5. Why it is unlikely
VISUAL: battery draining, data meter, magnifier (researchers), iPhone orange dot / Android green dot mockups.
21. So why don't experts believe your phone is recording everything you say for ads?
22. Recording and uploading hours of audio from billions of phones would drain batteries and burn data, and security researchers who watch app traffic every day would spot it.
23. Phones also warn you now. On an iPhone, an orange dot appears when an app is using the microphone. On Android, a green dot appears, and you can tap it to see which app it is.
24. And it would be a bad deal for advertisers anyway. They already know far more about you than one sentence at dinner could ever tell them.

## 6. The four routes ★ Max
VISUAL: Route cards 1-4: Wi-Fi + friends icons; Meta logo + pixel code snippet; shop email → profile match; map pins + gym icon + data broker; brain icon + "frequency illusion".
25. So how did the running shoe ad find you? Usually through one of four routes.
26. Route one: the people around you. Your friend looked up those shoes after dinner, on the same Wi-Fi. Facebook says it can estimate your location from your internet connection, even with location services off. So ad systems can guess who you spend time with.
27. Route two: the trail you leave. Many websites carry a tiny piece of code called the Meta Pixel. When you visit a page, it can report that visit back to Meta, even if you never click anything, and even if you don't have an account.
28. Shops can also upload customer email addresses and phone numbers, and Meta matches them to profiles. That's how a store you bought from once keeps finding you.
29. Route three: data brokers and your location. Some apps sell where you go. If your phone visits a gym three times a week, you look like someone who might buy running shoes.
30. Route four: your own brain. You see hundreds of ads every day and forget almost all of them. The one that matches a conversation is the one you remember. Psychologists call it the frequency illusion.
31. Put together, the system doesn't need to hear you. It predicts what you're about to want, sometimes before you even say it.
32. What about India? Our data protection law, the DPDP Act, finally has its rules in place since November twenty twenty-five.

## 7. India
VISUAL: Indian flag, DPDP Act clipping, timeline 2023 → Nov 2025 → 2027, consent toggle.
33. They roll out in phases until twenty twenty-seven. Companies will have to tell you clearly what data they collect and why, and let you withdraw your consent easily.
34. Until then, here is what you can do today, in about five minutes.

## 8. Five things to do ★ Max
VISUAL: Phone settings UI mockups for each step: microphone toggles, dot, Ask App Not to Track, off-Meta activity, wake word off.
35. One. Check which apps can use your microphone. On iPhone, go to Settings, Privacy and Security, Microphone. On Android, open the Privacy dashboard. Switch off anything that doesn't need it.
36. Two. Watch for the dot. If the orange or green dot appears when you're not on a call or recording, find out which app is using it.
37. Three. Limit ad tracking. On iPhone, choose "Ask App Not to Track". On Android, delete your advertising ID in Settings.
38. Four. On Facebook and Instagram, open your activity off Meta technologies, and disconnect the websites that report on you.
39. Five. If voice assistants worry you, turn off the wake word, and review or delete your saved voice recordings.
40. So, is your phone secretly listening to you? Almost certainly not, at least not for ads.
