# You're trapped in an elevator. What do you do? (DRAFT, awaiting approval)

## Verified facts (searched 2026-11-17; mostly building-safety and manufacturer guidance)
- Stay inside; the car is the safest place while stopped (Elevator Escalator Safety Foundation, via secondary sources). Do not pry doors or climb out: the car can start moving again and people have been hurt or killed (Otis, U Toronto Facilities, Technical Safety BC).
- Use the in-car phone / call or alarm button; if no answer, use your own phone; knock and shout so people on the next floor can tell the building (UIC, Northeastern, apartment/elevator-company guides).
- Common causes: building power outage, or the safety features kicked in; also a jammed door, a floor button that did not register, or the stop button pushed by mistake.
- Brakes hold the car safely until a technician arrives (secondary). Entrapments get priority service (Otis). Most entrapments are not life-threatening (Miami fire guidance).
- Many buildings call the fire department when the technician cannot arrive within 15 to 30 minutes (Cal State LA and Miami procedures; it varies, say "many buildings").
- Fire or medical emergency inside a stalled car gets an immediate fire department response (Escambia County Fire-Rescue). Do not use elevators in a fire unless the fire department says so (UIC).
- Safety systems: governor senses overspeed and triggers safeties that clamp the guide rails; buffers at the bottom of the shaft; safety brake invented in the 1850s (Otis, Elevator World). NOT claimed: "elevators can never fall". One source notes limits if all suspension ropes failed, hence modern standards. Script says "built with several independent safety systems".
- TO VERIFY (not found, so NOT in the script): air supply in a stopped cab; battery lowering in outages; breathing/claustrophobia tips.

### 1. The hook (0:00)
The lights flicker. The elevator jolts. And then it stops, between floors. The doors do not open. What do you do? The wrong move here can hurt you. The right one is surprisingly boring. Let's go through it.

### 2. Why this happens (0:25)
First, relax a little. Elevators stop for simple reasons. The most common is a power cut in the building. Sometimes the elevator's own safety system sees something odd and stops the car on purpose. And sometimes it is small: a door that did not close, a floor button that did not register, or the stop button pressed by accident.

### 3. Will it fall? (1:00)
Everyone's first fear is falling. Elevators are built for exactly this. A cable break does not mean a free fall. A speed governor watches how fast the car moves. If it goes too fast, it triggers safety clamps that grab the guide rails and stop the car. At the bottom of the shaft there are buffers as a backup. The first safety brake was shown off in the eighteen fifties. Several independent systems, so your job is not to save yourself from a fall.

### 4. Step one: check the simple things (1:35) [tip card]
Look at the panel. Is the stop button pushed in? Did you press your floor? Try pressing the door open button once. If nothing happens, you have done your part.

### 5. Step two: call for help (2:00) [tip card]
Press the call or alarm button. It connects to the building or a service centre, or rings a bell so people nearby know. Say where you are, and how many people are inside. If nobody answers, use your own phone. And if you have no signal, bang on the door and shout, so someone on the next floor can alert the building.

### 6. Step three: wait (2:40)
Now the boring part. Stay inside. The car is the safest place while it is stopped. Help is on the way. Entrapments get priority service, and many buildings call the fire department if the technician cannot arrive within fifteen to thirty minutes. It may feel like forever, but most entrapments are not life-threatening.

### 7. The big mistake (3:05)
Never pry the doors open. Never climb out through the roof like in the movies. The power can come back and the car can start moving again, with you halfway out. People have been seriously hurt this way. And if someone outside wants to help, tell them not to open the doors either. Let the trained people do it.

### 8. When it is an emergency (3:35)
If someone feels sick, or you see smoke, say so immediately, on the call button or on 911. A medical emergency or a fire gets an immediate fire department response. And in a fire, do not use the elevator in the first place, unless firefighters tell you to.

### 9. Recap (3:55)
Stay calm. Check the buttons. Call for help. Wait inside. Never open the doors. Follow for more.
